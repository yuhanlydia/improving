\begin{abstract}
A model that learns from its own outputs inherits more than their correctness: it inherits which solutions it produces. We study \emph{Looped Self-Distillation}, in which a model repeatedly generates a synthetic corpus and learns from it, and identify a consequential separation: correctness can improve while the breadth of correct implementations contracts. We introduce \method, a generation-time intervention that re-estimates loss-sensitive key/value geometry from a fixed reference anchor at each round and converts it into full-rank proximal spectral modulation. Every generated completion trains a single student, without rollout scoring or filtering; subsequent inference uses the native model. After five rounds of Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct on MBPP, \method\ retains $89.9\%$ of the initial model's 64-sample correct AST richness, compared with $66.4\%$ for plain self-distillation and $65.5\%$ for a subspace-projection control. It is the only arm in this comparison whose pass@64 point estimate exceeds the initial model's. The diversity advantage persists at matched correct-sample counts and is also observed on two transfer benchmarks, with dataset-dependent correctness trade-offs. These findings make correct-solution retention an explicit objective of repeated self-distillation and show that changing the generation geometry can improve what subsequent students retain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
An improving model is also becoming a different teacher. Repeatedly generating and learning from its own solutions can improve reasoning and coding performance \citep{zelikman2022star,singh2024restem}. The student's output distribution then matters twice: it determines what the model can solve now and what its next update can learn from. Higher correctness, however, need not make the next teacher a better source of diverse correct solutions.

We study \emph{Looped Self-Distillation under a fixed information budget}: the same evolving model repeatedly generates a corpus, learns from every raw completion, and becomes the next generator. \emph{No new annotations or external assessments of these rollouts enter training}: there is no verifier reward, judge-based ranking, or correctness-based filtering. \method\ reuses a reference anchor fixed before the loop for internal calibration; the anchor is never expanded with judged samples, and evaluation tests provide no training signal. This separates fixed reference supervision from ongoing rollout feedback.

What does this process retain within successful outputs? Mathematical problems can admit different proofs and planning tasks different valid plans; in code, generating multiple candidates supports search over alternative implementations \citep{li2022alphacode}. Executable tests make correctness measurable \citep{chen2021humaneval,liu2023evalplus}, but pass@$k$ does not identify which correct implementations a model produces. Holding total correctness probability fixed leaves the entire pass@$k$ curve unchanged under redistribution among correct solutions (Proposition~\ref{prop:breadth}).

Figure~\ref{fig:motivation} tracks this separation over five rounds. In the separate 64-sample evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:main}), Plain increases pass@1 from $37.94\%$ to $41.45\%$, while correct AST richness falls from $12.802$ to $8.506$ classes per task. The model becomes more accurate while losing a third of its measured correct implementation breadth. This connects concerns about recursive synthetic-data degradation \citep{shumailov2024collapse,gerstgrasser2024accumulating} to a regime where accuracy improves.

Iterative self-distillation has improved rationales and code-data synthesizers \citep{rao2023iterative,zhang2025scoder}, while SD-Zero and CRISP study evolving self-teachers \citep{he2026sdzero,sang2026crisp}. SSD learns from raw outputs through decoding-based distribution shaping \citep{zhang2026ssd}. Demonstration-conditioned self-distillation can also reduce solution diversity \citep{nicolicioiu2026diversity}. Our question is specific: \emph{can generation-time intervention preserve correct-solution breadth through repeated learning from unselected raw outputs?}

We introduce \method\ to address this question. Building on reference-gradient calibration \citep{hao2026spd}, it re-estimates the current student's loss-sensitive key/value geometry at each round. A full-rank proximal spectral gain retains a positive response in every local direction at finite strength. The temporarily modulated model generates the next corpus; native weights are then restored before a single LoRA learns from all raw completions \citep{hu2022lora}. The resulting student becomes the next generator, keeping the information source fixed while its geometry evolves.

After five rounds, \method\ preserves $89.9\%$ of initial 64-sample richness and improves pass@64 by $2.2$ percentage points over Plain. Its richness advantage persists at matched correct-sample counts and is measured through native inference, after removing the intervention. Pass@1 is $1.12$ points below Plain and $2.39$ points above the initial model. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Solution retention under a fixed information budget.} We separate correctness from conditional implementation breadth and document their divergent evolution across five rounds without rollout-based external feedback.
\item \textbf{A generation intervention for the loop.} \method\ combines a fixed reference anchor, per-round geometry estimation, and full-rank proximal spectral modulation before single-student learning.
\item \textbf{Evidence at complementary endpoints.} We evaluate total-budget richness, correct-count-matched richness, and multi-sample success, with projection and SSD comparisons and frozen-student transfer to HumanEval+ and APPS Intro.
\end{enumerate}

\section{What should a self-distillation loop retain?}
\label{sec:problem}
For a task $x$, let $v_x(y)\in\{0,1\}$ denote passing its recorded verifier, and let $\phi(y)$ identify an implementation class. In coding, $\phi$ is a normalized AST fingerprint. Write $a_\theta(x)=\Pr_{\pi_\theta}[v_x(Y)=1]$ and, when $a_\theta(x)>0$,
\begin{equation}
q_{\theta,j}(x)=\Pr_{\pi_\theta}[\phi(Y)=j\mid v_x(Y)=1],\qquad
\Pr[v_x(Y)=1,\phi(Y)=j]=a_\theta(x)q_{\theta,j}(x).
\label{eq:factor}
\end{equation}
The first factor is how frequently the model succeeds; the second is how it distributes its successes. We suppress $x$ below and assume independent draws from a fixed decoding policy.

\begin{proposition}[Correctness does not identify correct-solution breadth]
\label{prop:breadth}
For fixed $a>0$, pass@$k=1-(1-a)^k$ is independent of $q$. Expected correct-class counts under $k$ total draws and $b$ correct draws are, respectively,
\begin{equation}
C_k(a,q)=\sum_j[1-(1-aq_j)^k],\qquad
D_b(q)=\sum_j[1-(1-q_j)^b].
\label{eq:occupancy}
\end{equation}
Distributions with identical pass@$k$ for every $k$ can have different $D_b$ for any $b\ge2$.
\end{proposition}
For example, at $a=0.4$, concentrating all correct mass on one class gives $D_4=1$, whereas distributing it uniformly across four classes gives $D_4=2.734375$. Both policies have pass@4 equal to $0.8704$. These are elementary occupancy identities, not a model-specific assumption. They explain why correctness alone cannot diagnose contraction within correct outputs.

We use descriptive names: \emph{correct AST richness@$k$} for $C_k$ and \emph{correct-count-matched AST richness@$b$} for $D_b$. Standard rarefaction estimators compute them from finite sample pools \citep{hurlbert1971nonconcept}; definitions and proofs appear in Appendix~\ref{app:metrics}. $C_k$ answers what breadth a fixed generation budget buys. $D_b$ asks whether a difference remains after equalizing successful draws. Conditional comparisons use tasks eligible for both methods and report their number. Neither metric identifies semantic algorithm equivalence.

For a sequence of students $\theta_0,\ldots,\theta_T$, the \emph{retention profile} records $(\mathrm{pass@}k,C_k,D_b)$ under a common evaluation protocol. Relative richness retention is $R_{t,k}=C_k(\theta_t)/C_k(\theta_0)$ when defined. This is retention of expected class \emph{count}, not identity overlap with the initial classes. It measures a finite-budget repertoire rather than the total support of the model's distribution.

\section{Looped Self-Distillation with a fixed reference anchor}
\label{sec:loop}
Let $\cQ$ be a fixed set of synthesis prompts and $\theta_t$ the current native student. A generation constructor $\mathcal G$ and a learner $\mathcal L$ define one round:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde\pi_t=\mathcal G(\theta_t;\cA),\qquad
\cD_t=\{(x,Y_{x,s}):x\in\cQ,\ Y_{x,s}\sim\widetilde\pi_t\}_{s=1}^{S},\qquad
\theta_{t+1}=\mathcal L(\theta_t,\cD_t).
\label{eq:loop}
\end{equation}
Here $\cA=\{(x_i,y_i^\star)\}$ is optional calibration information fixed before the loop. Plain and decoding-based self-distillation ignore $\cA$; \method\ uses reference completions to shape generation. Our fixed-information setting admits no new annotations or rollout assessments into training, and the learner retains every generated record. It warm-starts from $\theta_t$ and updates a single LoRA adapter \citep{hu2022lora}. The merged student becomes the next generator's starting point. Historical corpora are not accumulated.

\paragraph{Why loop?}
A one-round evaluation measures a teacher--student transition. A loop additionally asks what happens when the resulting student becomes the teacher again. It exposes whether an immediate gain provides a useful distribution for subsequent learning. This is an operational setting for studying repeated self-distillation, building on iterative self-training; monotonic self-improvement is an empirical question, not part of its definition. The term also distinguishes an outer \emph{learning} loop, with changing parameters, from recurrent-depth transformers that repeat computation within inference \citep{dehghani2019universal}.

\paragraph{Where supervision enters.}
\method\ repeatedly uses the \emph{same} reference anchor to recalibrate the evolving model. The anchor is external supervision fixed before the loop; the geometry derived from it changes each round. Generated completions are never scored, ranked, filtered, or reweighted by a verifier, judge, or strategy label. Test outcomes are used only for evaluation. This distinguishes fixed calibration guidance from ongoing assessment of synthetic samples. It does not make the method fully unsupervised. The student objective is sequence-level cross-entropy on self-generated data, rather than reward optimization or a demonstration-conditioned teacher's token-level KL loss.

\section{SPECTRUM: proximal spectral modulation}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Reference geometry without assigning solution strategies}
For anchor example $(x_i,y_i^\star)$, let $\ell_i(\theta_t)$ be mean negative log likelihood over its completion targets. Prompt and padding targets are masked. For selected K/V module $\ell$, collect $g_{iu}^{(\ell)}$, the gradient of this scalar loss with respect to the module's native linear output at every nonpadding position $u$. Following reference-loss-gradient calibration of K/V directions \citep{hao2026spd}, compute
\begin{equation}
C_{t,\ell}=\frac{1}{M_\ell}\sum_{i,u\,\mathrm{nonpad}}
 g_{iu}^{(\ell)}g_{iu}^{(\ell)\top},\qquad
\Cbar_{t,\ell}=C_{t,\ell}/\lambda_{\max}(C_{t,\ell}).
\label{eq:geometry}
\end{equation}
$M_\ell$ counts the included positions, including those with zero gradient. Collection includes prompt positions: masking their loss targets does not remove their influence on completion loss. The statistic is an uncentered gradient second moment, with normalized spectrum in $[0,1]$.

High sensitivity indicates that the reference loss responds strongly to a direction. Low sensitivity on a finite anchor does not establish that the direction is useless for other valid implementations. We therefore use the spectrum continuously. There is no assignment of ten solutions to ten subspaces, no strategy annotation, and no adapter pool.

For a unit direction $v$, the quadratic form $v^\top C_{t,\ell}v$ is the mean squared directional derivative $(g_{iu}^{(\ell)\top}v)^2$. It measures sensitivity, not a reward or a signed improvement direction. Normalization removes the overall gradient scale and makes $\tau$ control relative attenuation. Re-estimating this statistic matters because the student parameters, activations, and reference losses change after learning. Reusing the anchor holds its information budget fixed while measuring sensitivity in the current model; fixing the operator instead would reuse geometry estimated under earlier parameters.

\subsection{A full-rank proximal gain}
For row activation $h\in\R^{1\times d}$, define the modulated state by
\begin{equation}
\argmin_z\left\{\frac12\|z-h\|_2^2+
\frac\tau2 z(\I-\Cbar)z^\top\right\}=hT_\tau,
\qquad T_\tau=[\I+\tau(\I-\Cbar)]^{-1},\quad \tau\ge0.
\label{eq:prox}
\end{equation}
This is a classical quadratic proximal map \citep{parikh2014proximal}, applied here to the generation geometry of a self-distillation loop. The first term limits departure from the current state; the second attenuates directions less emphasized by the reference statistic. If $\Cbar=U\diag(\mu_j)U^\top$, its gain is
\begin{equation}
T_\tau=U\diag\!\left(\frac{1}{1+\tau(1-\mu_j)}\right)U^\top.
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
The maximal-sensitivity directions retain unit gain, while every other direction retains a positive response at finite $\tau$. Modulation strength controls this departure continuously; it is not a prescribed count of solution modes.

For illustration, normalized eigenvalues $(1,\tfrac12,0)$ at $\tau=1$ produce gains $(1,\tfrac23,\tfrac12)$. The least-sensitive component is attenuated but remains available to downstream computation. This treatment is useful when an anchor covers only some valid implementations: absence of measured sensitivity need not become an irreversible local deletion. It supplies a design rationale for retention, rather than assuming that eigenvectors correspond to named algorithms.

\begin{proposition}[Local distinguishability and calibration stability]
\label{prop:prox}
For $\Cbar\succeq0$ with eigenvalues in $[0,1]$ and finite $\tau\ge0$, Equation~\eqref{eq:prox} has a unique solution. Its gains lie in $[1/(1+\tau),1]$, and
\begin{equation}
\frac{\|\delta\|_2}{1+\tau}\le\|\delta T_\tau\|_2\le\|\delta\|_2,
\qquad \|T_\tau-\I\|_2\le\frac{\tau}{1+\tau}.
\label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
For normalized PSD statistics $A,B$ with spectra in $[0,1]$,
$\|T_\tau(A)-T_\tau(B)\|_2\le\tau\|A-B\|_2$.
\end{proposition}
The operator thus avoids exact local erasure and is continuous in its normalized geometry without an eigengap assumption. These are properties of a local linear map; preservation of semantic solution classes through attention, decoding, and training remains an empirical question. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} proves the bounds and states their scope.

\subsection{From a temporary generator to a native student}
For $h=xW^\top+b^\top$, fold the row operator into $W'=T_\tau^\top W$ and $b'=T_\tau^\top b$. Then $xW'^\top+b'^\top=hT_\tau$. Folding acts before rotary position transformations and key normalization, and introduces no extra activation hook. After generation, restore the original weights and train on all raw completions, including unsuccessful, empty, and duplicate records.

Let $z=(x,y)$ be a tokenized training record, $\mathcal T(z)$ its nonpadding causal targets, and $\Delta$ the effective LoRA weight update. The learner optimizes
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_t(\Delta)=-\frac{1}{|\cD_t|}\sum_{z\in\cD_t}
\frac{1}{|\mathcal T(z)|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal T(z)}
\log p_{\theta_t+\Delta}(z_u\mid z_{<u}).
\label{eq:student}
\end{equation}
Our recorded SFT loss includes prompt targets, whereas calibration masks them. Every record has the same example weight; the spectral gain changes the sampled corpus, not a sample's loss coefficient. Thus frequently generated implementations contribute through their empirical frequency, with no routing of solutions to separate adapters. After the fixed training schedule, merging $\Delta_t$ yields $\theta_{t+1}$. Its geometry is recalibrated from the same $\cA$ next round. Native evaluation uses neither the gain nor the anchor, so the measured endpoint is the distribution learned by the student.

The K and V interventions play complementary roles: changing keys alters query--key compatibility, while changing values alters the information aggregated by attention. Their effect depends on the input and current hidden state, beyond a global temperature applied to output logits. Across rounds, only the learned student is carried forward. Restoring native weights prevents direct composition of successive spectral gains; the next operator is estimated afresh from the updated student. This construction makes the synthetic corpus the channel through which a temporary geometric intervention can persist in later behavior.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
\subsection{Protocol and research questions}
The main experiment uses Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct \citep{hui2024qwen25coder} and MBPP's full test split of 500 tasks \citep{austin2021mbpp}. After removing three exact normalized train/test prompt overlaps, the training pool contains 291 synthesis tasks, 50 fixed calibration tasks, and 30 validation tasks. These sets are disjoint. Each of five rounds generates 16 raw completions per synthesis task and runs one LoRA epoch: 4,656 records per round, 23,280 in total. The completed setting uses $\tau=1$, middle and last decoder-layer K/V modules, and LoRA rank 8. It does not choose $\tau$ anew from test outcomes.

All native students use common evaluation decoding: temperature $0.8$, top-$p$ $0.95$, and no top-$k$ truncation. The historical trajectory has 16 samples per task per round; a separate final supplement evaluates the initial and round-five checkpoints with 64 samples per task. These pools are kept distinct. The main training seed is 43; confidence intervals resample evaluation tasks, not training seeds. We report available results rather than treating configured runs as completed evidence. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives the exact losses, prompts, and budgets.

We ask four questions: \textbf{RQ1}, does spectral generation improve breadth retained by a native student? \textbf{RQ2}, does the benefit survive matching correct-sample counts and changing sample budget? \textbf{RQ3}, what trade-offs arise under a projection control and decoding-based SSD? \textbf{RQ4}, does the learned effect transfer beyond the training benchmark?

\subsection{RQ1: Can the learned student retain more correct-solution breadth?}
\label{sec:rq1}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives the final 64-sample comparison. \method\ retains $11.510$ correct AST classes per task, versus $8.506$ for Plain: a $35.3\%$ relative increase, or a paired difference of $3.004$ classes with 95\% task-bootstrap interval $[2.568,3.494]$. Relative to the initial model, this is $89.9\%$ versus $66.4\%$ richness retention. The result is partial preservation: \method\ still loses $10.1\%$ of the initial richness.

The accuracy profile explains what this retention buys. \method\ solves 363 tasks at 64 samples, compared with 352 for Plain, a pass@64 gain of $2.2$ points $[0.4,4.2]$. Its point estimate also exceeds the initial model's 360 tasks, although that difference is inconclusive ($+0.6$ points, $[-1.8,3.0]$). The recorded one-point noninferiority criterion against Plain is therefore not met. The 16-sample trajectory connects this endpoint to repeated learning. Plain's correct AST richness declines from $4.016$ to $3.090$, while \method\ ends at $3.606$. \method\ has higher richness than Plain at every recorded post-training round. The corresponding relative retentions are $76.9\%$ and $89.8\%$ at this smaller budget. All these measurements concern native students; the generation intervention is absent during their evaluation.

\subsection{RQ2: Is the gain explained by more successful samples?}
\label{sec:rq2}
Correct-sample matching isolates variety within successful outputs. At four correct draws, \method\ exceeds Plain by $0.295$ expected AST classes, with interval $[0.249,0.343]$, on 316 common eligible tasks. At eight and sixteen correct draws, the paired gains are $0.780$ and $1.816$, on 296 and 254 tasks (Figure~\ref{fig:budget}c). Thus the gain in $C_{64}$ cannot be explained solely by obtaining more correct draws. Eligibility changes with $b$; these conditional estimates do not describe all 500 tasks.

The budget curves reveal complementary behavior. At $k=1$, $C_1$ is correctness itself and favors Plain. Matched-correct gains also grow with $b$, showing that the difference extends beyond a single extra rare structure. These findings support using success and conditional richness together; neither alone fully describes a generator's value for the next loop.

\subsection{RQ3: What does the generation design change?}
\label{sec:rq3}
We replace the proximal gain by a top-half rank projector as a direct design control, using the same reference geometry and learning schedule. This control follows the subspace-projection construction of \citet{hao2026spd}; it is a declared reconstruction with our completion-loss and insertion-point choices, not an official reproduction. Its round-five $C_{64}$ is $8.388$ (65.5\% retention), versus $11.510$ for \method. The soft gain improves richness by $3.122$ classes and pass@64 by $2.4$ points while lowering pass@1 by $1.43$ points. This contrast supports retaining a continuous local response; it does not isolate spectral orientation from attenuation strength.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Iterative self-training and self-distillation.}
STaR and ReST-EM establish repeated generation and learning as a route to improving reasoning from self-produced solutions \citep{zelikman2022star,singh2024restem}. Iteration also exists explicitly within self-distillation: \citet{rao2023iterative} improve contexts and rationales using critics and filtering, while SCoder develops a code-data synthesizer through multiple checkpoints, scoring, and sample selection \citep{zhang2025scoder}. These works motivate studying the student as a future data source. Our loop exposes the evolution of that source under a fixed prompt and reference budget, measuring which correct implementation breadth survives each complete corpus-generation/student-update cycle. It concerns outer parameter learning, whereas recurrent-depth transformers repeat computation within a forward process \citep{dehghani2019universal}.

\paragraph{The source of self-distillation feedback.}
Self-generated targets can support different learning objectives. SD-Zero turns reward-conditioned self-revision into dense supervision and synchronizes teachers during self-evolution \citep{he2026sdzero}. CRISP periodically refreshes an instruction-conditioned self-teacher and uses on-policy token-level KL training to compress reasoning, without correctness filtering \citep{sang2026crisp}. SSD trains on raw code samples whose distribution is shaped by temperature and truncation, and analyzes the tension between precision and exploration \citep{zhang2026ssd}. \method\ shares sequence-level learning from raw outputs with SSD, while deriving its generation intervention from fixed reference-loss geometry. Consequently, its supervision budget and mechanism differ from both purely unlabeled self-training and demonstration-conditioned teacher feedback. Avoiding rollout selection and refreshing a teacher are existing ingredients; the retention question concerns their repeated downstream effect.

\paragraph{Diversity under recursive learning.}
\citet{nicolicioiu2026diversity} identify reduced functional and semantic diversity in self-distillation conditioned on sampled correct demonstrations. Their analysis connects the optimal-policy bias to compatibility between a rollout and its demonstration. Our empirical loop omits this feedback mechanism and evaluates structural richness within correct outputs after repeated raw-output learning. UA-RL takes another route by rewarding rare solution strategies \citep{hu2026uarlacl}; \method\ does not score or label generated strategies. More broadly, recursive model-collapse studies show distributional degradation under synthetic training, and retaining accumulated data can mitigate it \citep{shumailov2024collapse,gerstgrasser2024accumulating}. Here the endpoint is conditional on correctness: implementation breadth can decline while accuracy improves. Jointly reporting pass@$k$, $C_k$, and $D_b$ distinguishes successful-draw frequency from breadth within those draws, while keeping the historical-data policy fixed.

\paragraph{Spectral control and retained student behavior.}
Spectral activation editing uses internal geometry to control model outputs \citep{qiu2024sea}. Capability-selective self-policy distillation constructs reference-gradient K/V subspaces for generation \citep{hao2026spd}. We build on this calibration principle and apply a full-rank proximal response, with per-round geometry estimation and subsequent learning in one native student. Quadratic proximal maps and their closed forms are classical \citep{parikh2014proximal}. Their role here is to regulate sensitivity-based attenuation without setting a local direction's gain to zero. The operator bounds motivate the design; longitudinal, correct-count-matched student measurements establish the observed retention effect. This separation distinguishes a mathematically controlled intervention from an assumed semantic-diversity guarantee.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The completed study uses one 1.5B backbone and one training seed, with two frozen-student transfer benchmarks. Task-bootstrap intervals characterize evaluation-task uncertainty rather than training variability. AST fingerprints measure implementation structure, not audited algorithm identities; passing finite tests is an operational correctness criterion. The findings therefore concern measured code repertoires, with extension to proofs or plans left open.

The fixed reference anchor supplies external information. Plain does not isolate its contribution from the operator; the projection control shares the anchor, but directional and strength-matched controls are absent. Equal record counts also give unequal token budgets: \method\ generates 9.9\% more training tokens than Plain (Appendix~\ref{app:resources}). Full rank is a local property, and neither global diversity preservation nor monotonic improvement follows from it. The lower pass@1 and APPS task coverage identify boundaries of the observed retention benefit.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Looped Self-Distillation makes each student the source of its next training distribution. Our study shows that this process can improve correctness while contracting correct implementation breadth. \method\ combines a fixed reference anchor with evolving loss-sensitive geometry and a full-rank proximal gain. After five rounds it retains 89.9\% of initial 64-sample richness, compared with 66.4\% for Plain; matched-correct analyses locate a benefit inside successful outputs, and native inference shows that it survives student learning.

Future work can connect these endpoints more directly by measuring immediate generator changes and their transmission into successive students. Matched-token studies, fixed-geometry controls, and alternative spectral orientations would isolate the contribution of recalibration and directional structure. Extending the loop across model families and to verifiable proofs or plans would test whether retaining multiple valid solutions is a general design principle for repeated self-improvement.

\section{Metrics, estimators, and probability analysis}
\label{app:metrics}
\subsection{Correctness and conditional implementation distributions}
For a fixed task, let $V=v_x(Y)$ and $J=\phi(Y)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:factor} is simply $\Pr(V=1,J=j)=\Pr(V=1)\Pr(J=j\mid V=1)$. If $a=0$, unconditional correct richness is zero and $q$ is undefined. We do not assign a fabricated conditional distribution to a task with no observed successes. For countably many classes the occupancy sums converge, because $1-(1-p)^k\le kp$ gives $C_k\le ka$ and $D_b\le b$. These quantities count expected observed classes at the stated budget, not all possible implementations.

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:breadth}.}
Let $I_j$ indicate observing at least one correct output from class $j$ in $k$ independent draws. Its expectation is $1-(1-aq_j)^k$. Linearity of expectation gives $C_k$. The same argument for $b$ draws from the conditional correct distribution yields $D_b$. All $k$ draws fail with probability $(1-a)^k$, so pass@$k$ depends only on $a$. For $q=(1,0,\ldots)$, $D_b=1$. For uniform mass on $J\ge2$ classes, $D_b=J[1-(1-1/J)^b]>1$ when $b\ge2$. Hence identical correctness at every budget does not identify conditional breadth.\hfill$\square$

More generally, spreading mass from a larger to a smaller conditional class, without reversing their order, cannot decrease $D_b$: $f(q)=1-(1-q)^b$ is concave for $b\ge2$. The same applies to $C_k$ at fixed $a$ and $k\ge2$. This describes a change in a probability distribution; it does not assert that the spectral operator induces that change in every network.

\subsection{Finite-sample estimators}
Suppose a task has $n$ generated outputs, $m$ of which pass its verifier, and correct-class counts $m_j$ with $\sum_jm_j=m$. We compute
\begin{align}
\widehat{\mathrm{pass@}k}&=1-\frac{\binom{n-m}{k}}{\binom nk}, &&1\le k\le n,\label{eq:passest}\\
\widehat C_k&=\sum_j\left[1-\frac{\binom{n-m_j}{k}}{\binom nk}\right], &&1\le k\le n,\label{eq:cest}\\
\widehat D_b&=\sum_j\left[1-\frac{\binom{m-m_j}{b}}{\binom mb}\right], &&1\le b\le m.\label{eq:dest}
\end{align}
We set $\binom uv=0$ for nonnegative integers $u<v$. Equation~\eqref{eq:passest} is the standard pass@$k$ estimator \citep{chen2021humaneval}. Equations~\eqref{eq:cest}--\eqref{eq:dest} are rarefaction estimators \citep{hurlbert1971nonconcept}. A uniformly chosen subset of $k$ outputs misses correct class $j$ with probability $\binom{n-m_j}{k}/\binom nk$. Summing its complement gives the exact expected subset richness conditional on the recorded pool; under independent sampling it estimates the corresponding population quantity. Conditioning on correct outputs gives the analogous calculation for $D_b$.

At $k=n$, $\widehat C_n$ is the number of observed correct classes. At $k=1$, $\widehat C_1=m/n=\widehat{\mathrm{pass@}1}$. At $b=1$, $D_1=1$ wherever defined. Thus neither $C_1$ nor $D_1$ distinguishes how probability is distributed among multiple correct classes. The plotted budgets are those present in the archived estimates; we do not reconstruct a missing pass@32 value by interpolation.

\subsection{AST fingerprints and concentration}
Python programs are extracted using the recorded first-fenced-block rule. The primary fingerprint parses the extracted program and conservatively normalizes its AST. Formatting and docstrings are removed; local identifiers are normalized only when scope handling is unambiguous. Function names, external identifiers, literals, and uncertain scopes are retained. Consequently, distinct fingerprints can reflect structural implementation variants within one algorithm. Conversely, a fingerprint does not establish functional equivalence beyond the recorded tests. Parse failures do not silently become new correct classes. Secondary exact-program and control-flow fingerprints provide different granularities.

For Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}c, the saved correct-output Simpson statistic is
\begin{equation}
\widehat S=1-\frac{\sum_j m_j(m_j-1)}{m(m-1)},\qquad m\ge2.
\end{equation}
It estimates the probability that two independently drawn correct outputs fall in different classes. Its aggregate uses tasks with enough correct samples and complete labels. It complements richness by emphasizing concentration, while sharing the limitations of the class definition. Exact-program, AST, and control-flow metrics need not move equally; the coarser control-flow comparisons do not establish a uniform increase in algorithmic families.

\subsection{Task aggregation and uncertainty}
Pass@$k$ and $C_k$ are macro-averaged over the full task set. A marginal $D_b$ mean uses $\mathcal E_b(M)=\{x:m_x(M)\ge b\}$ for model $M$ and reports its size. A paired contrast between $M_1,M_2$ averages task-level differences on $\mathcal E_b(M_1)\cap\mathcal E_b(M_2)$. Subtracting marginal means with different eligible sets is not this paired effect. Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}b pairs each round with initialization; its eligible population can change between rounds. It is a pointwise trajectory, not a single fixed cohort.

Archived MBPP intervals use 2,000 task-bootstrap resamples. For the new analysis of saved transfer metrics, we fix bootstrap seed 20260924 and also use 2,000 resamples, pairing by exact task ID and conditioning on common eligibility. Percentile endpoints are the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The analysis script records the means, intervals, populations, and input hashes. These intervals describe evaluation-task uncertainty for the saved checkpoints. They do not measure independent training-seed variation, prove generality across architectures, or treat rounds as independent replications. They are pointwise exploratory intervals without multiplicity adjustment.

\section{Operator derivation and mathematical scope}
\label{app:proofs}
\subsection{The calibration loss}
Let $\mathcal T_i$ contain the nonpadding completion targets of reference $i$. The scalar differentiated is
\begin{equation}
\ell_i(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal T_i|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal T_i}
\log\pi_\theta(y_{iu}^\star\mid x_i,y_{i,<u}^\star).
\end{equation}
It is computed per example before collecting gradients. Every nonpadding K/V-output position contributes to Equation~\eqref{eq:geometry}, including prompt positions and zero-gradient positions. Thus the loss mask and gradient-position mask are distinct. A large eigenvalue of $C$ represents mean squared directional sensitivity. Opposite signs both contribute positively, so this statistic does not establish signed update agreement or correctness of a generated sample. Empty or zero-signal calibration is rejected; $C/\lambda_{\max}(C)$ otherwise would be undefined.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:prox}}
The Hessian of Equation~\eqref{eq:prox} is $H=\I+\tau(\I-\Cbar)$, with eigenvalues $1+\tau(1-\mu_j)\in[1,1+\tau]$. It is positive definite, so the objective has a unique minimizer. Its first-order condition is $zH=h$, giving $z=hH^{-1}$. Diagonalizing $H$ yields Equation~\eqref{eq:gain}. Since $T_\tau$ is symmetric positive definite, its singular values equal its gains, proving the two-sided norm bound. Also,
\begin{equation}
\|T_\tau-\I\|_2=\max_j\frac{\tau(1-\mu_j)}{1+\tau(1-\mu_j)}
\le\frac{\tau}{1+\tau}.
\end{equation}
The condition number is at most $1+\tau$, and $T_0=\I$.
For normalized statistics $A,B$, the resolvent identity gives
\begin{equation}
T_\tau(A)-T_\tau(B)=\tau T_\tau(A)(A-B)T_\tau(B).
\end{equation}
Both outer factors have operator norm at most one, which proves the stability bound without an eigenvector or eigengap assumption.\hfill$\square$

This last result applies to normalized statistics. If raw PSD matrices $C,D$ both satisfy $\lambda_{\max}\ge\gamma>0$, Weyl's inequality and a triangle inequality additionally give
\begin{equation}
\left\|\frac{C}{\lambda_{\max}(C)}-\frac{D}{\lambda_{\max}(D)}\right\|_2
\le\frac{2\|C-D\|_2}{\gamma}.
\end{equation}
Combining the bounds yields a local perturbation bound $2\tau\|C-D\|_2/\gamma$. It is not a sampling-concentration theorem for the calibration estimator; it explicitly requires a nonvanishing normalization scale.

\subsection{Folding, finite precision, and projection control}
For row activations,
\begin{equation}
(xW^\top+b^\top)T=x(T^\top W)^\top+(T^\top b)^\top.
\end{equation}
This algebra justifies temporary weight folding. The implementation computes products in float32 and casts back to the model dtype; exact invertibility statements concern real arithmetic. The insertion point is the native K/V linear output before rotary position operations and any subsequent key normalization. Grouped-query dimensions come from each selected linear module's actual output dimension. The original weights are saved and restored before learning. Folding is an execution equivalence, not a separate claim of methodological novelty.

The projection design uses $P_r=U_{1:r}U_{1:r}^\top$, with $r$ equal to half the module output dimension in the completed run. It preserves retained directions and nulls the remainder. This differs from $T_\tau$ in both its spectrum and effective strength; the comparison does not constitute a distance-from-identity-matched experiment. At $\tau=1$, \method's gains lie in $[1/2,1]$. We do not claim to have measured the effect of random eigenvectors, isotropic attenuation, fixed geometry, or a $\tau$ sweep. Those controls are implementation options, not results in this paper.

\subsection{Why the local result is not a semantic theorem}
Distinct activations remain distinct after an invertible local map. Subsequent attention, nonlinear layers, vocabulary projection, stochastic decoding, and finite-data optimization can still map them to the same output class. A full-rank gain therefore cannot guarantee a particular value of $C_k$ or $D_b$. Likewise, raw self-training has no unconditional improvement guarantee: in the idealized conditional setting where the sampling policy exactly equals the model distribution, the expected self-score gradient is zero at the generating parameters. The practical loop changes the sampling distribution, uses finite corpora and optimization, and in \method\ uses fixed reference information. The empirical measurements are needed to establish the direction of change.

\section{Algorithm, information flow, and protocol}
\label{app:protocol}
\subsection{A complete round}
The following sequence states the exact separation of generator and learner.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Fix synthesis prompts $\cQ$, reference anchor $\cA$, held-out evaluation tasks, decoding settings, and the initial checkpoint $\theta_0$ before the loop.
\item At round $t$, calculate reference-completion loss under $\theta_t$, collect selected K/V-output gradients, and form $\Cbar_{t,\ell}$ on the unchanged anchor.
\item Construct $T_{t,\ell}$ with the fixed strength $\tau$ and temporarily fold the operators into the current checkpoint's native linear weights.
\item Sample $S$ completions for each synthesis prompt. Keep every raw record. No execution outcome, reward, uniqueness judgment, or AST label determines membership or weight in $\cD_t$.
\item Restore the original $\theta_t$ weights. Fit one LoRA adapter on $\cD_t$, merge it into the base weights, and save $\theta_{t+1}$.
\item Evaluate $\theta_{t+1}$ through common native inference, storing all outputs and verification labels. These labels do not affect the next training round. Repeat from step 2 using $\theta_{t+1}$.
\end{enumerate}
The anchor itself is never expanded with newly judged samples. It is reused as supervision at every calibration step, so ``fixed'' describes the information source rather than an initialization-only operation. In the completed study, there is no paired intervention-on generator diagnostic at every round; the native endpoints establish retained differences after learning, not the fraction of an immediate generator benefit transferred by SFT.

Exact normalized prompt overlaps with held-out prompts are removed before partitioning the training pool; the recorded excluded training IDs are 602, 704, and 872. The resulting sizes are 291 synthesis, 50 calibration, 30 validation, and 500 evaluation tasks. A Python signature stub is extracted mechanically from the reference AST to specify the interface. Its implementation and evaluation assertions are not placed in the synthesis/evaluation prompt. The calibration partition supplies complete reference solutions.

\subsection{What the student optimizes}
Let $s$ denote the concatenated tokenized prompt and generated completion, and let $\mathcal T(s)$ contain its nonpadding causal targets. The recorded microbatch-one schedule accumulates example-level token-mean losses,
\begin{equation}
\ell_s(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal T(s)|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal T(s)}\log\pi_\theta(s_u\mid s_{<u}),
\qquad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{SFT}}=\frac{1}{|\cD_t|}\sum_{s\in\cD_t}\ell_s(\theta).
\end{equation}
Gradient accumulation follows the recorded minibatch schedule. Including prompt targets and averaging each example by its own length are explicit choices, distinct from completion-only calibration and globally token-weighted corpus optimization. There are no sample-specific weights based on correctness, generated length, or source subspace. Truncation at the fixed context limit is a sequence-length constraint, not quality filtering. The completed training summaries record zero truncated training examples.

\subsection{Information budgets and comparators}
Plain and SSD do not use the reference anchor. \method\ and the projection control use the same 50 references at each calibration step. Hence their direct comparison controls access to calibration information, whereas comparing either to Plain includes that difference in information use. No completed anchor-only SFT control isolates this factor. SSD here adapts the published decoding idea to the small-model MBPP budget; it does not reproduce the original larger-scale results. UA-RL, which uses rewards for rare strategies, is discussed as related work but is not a completed numerical comparator in this archive.

\section{Resources and realized sample lengths}

\section{Positioning and evidence boundaries}
\label{app:positioning}
\paragraph{Sequence-level distillation versus token-feedback OPSD.}
The common phrase ``learning from one's own outputs'' covers multiple objectives. In sequence-level self-distillation, sampled tokens become cross-entropy targets. In demonstration-conditioned on-policy distillation, student rollouts receive dense token feedback from a teacher with privileged context. The present implementation is the former. Its sampling policy is derived from the current model, but temporarily changes selected weights; ``on-policy'' should not erase this generator--student distinction. We use Looped Self-Distillation as a precise outer-process description.

\paragraph{Relation to earlier iterative methods.}
Iterative self-distillation is not introduced for the first time here. The context/rationale study of \citet{rao2023iterative} includes iterative self-imitation and examines quality and diversity. SCoder progressively improves a data synthesizer using multiple checkpoints and scoring/selection, and discusses declining diversity \citep{zhang2025scoder}. CRISP demonstrates recurring teacher updates without correctness-based sample selection \citep{sang2026crisp}. These works prevent defining novelty solely through recurrence or the absence of a verifier filter. Our narrower contribution joins conditional correct-implementation retention, a fixed-anchor internal generation intervention, and repeated measurements of the native learned students.

\paragraph{Relation to demonstration-conditioned diversity loss.}
\citet{nicolicioiu2026diversity} analyze self-distillation with sampled correct demonstrations, including feedback bias and diversity changes over training. Their mechanism should not be described as having no temporal iteration. Our five outer rounds repeatedly generate a complete raw corpus and fit a new native student without a correct-demonstration teacher. The occurrence of contraction in this setting shows that rollout filtering or that particular teacher-feedback construction is not necessary for the measured phenomenon. It does not identify a unique causal source of contraction, nor establish that all self-distillation schemes must contract.

\paragraph{Completed and excluded evidence.}
The manuscript uses the full MBPP seed-43 five-round archive, its separate final 64-sample supplement, the SSD seed-43 continuation, and saved HumanEval+/APPS transfer records. Earlier 64-task pilot results are not pooled with the full benchmark. Four-task smoke runs are software checks, not research evidence. Configured multi-seed confirmation, larger models, additional architectures, further datasets, UA-RL, and unexecuted geometry controls are excluded from numerical tables. Their existence as runnable code does not establish measured performance. All figures and additional intervals in this revision are analyses of already recorded results.
